INDIANA SUPREME COURT March 12, 2008

Ex Parte Jenkins, 881 N.E.2d 1005

SYLLABUS

Defendant was convicted in the Superior Court of felony murder based on the death of his co-felon, who was shot by police during their flight from a robbery. Defendant appealed. The Supreme Court held that the proximate cause theory of felony murder applies when a defendant's conduct sets in motion a chain of events resulting in death, even if the fatal shot was fired by a non-participant.

Justice SULLIVAN delivered the opinion of the Court.

This case requires us to reexamine the scope of the felony murder rule under Indiana Code § 35-42-1-1(2) specifically concerning the "proximate cause" theory of liability when the victim is a co-participant in the underlying felony and the fatal act is committed by a third party.

I. Factual Background

The facts are largely undisputed. On the evening of November 4, 2004, Jenkins and his accomplice, Vance, armed with handguns, entered a convenience store with the intent to commit robbery. An off-duty police officer, present in the store, intervened. A firefight ensued. Vance was struck by a bullet fired by the officer and died at the scene. Jenkins fled but was apprehended shortly thereafter.

II. Discussion

Jenkins argues that he cannot be held liable for felony murder because he did not pull the trigger, and the person killed was his co-felon, not an innocent bystander. He urges this Court to adopt an "agency theory" of felony murder.

We decline the invitation. Indiana has long adhered to the proximate cause theory of felony murder. As we stated in Palmer v. State, 741 N.E.2d 1245 (Ind. 2001), it is not necessary that the defendant's own act be the direct cause of death; it is sufficient if the defendant's conduct during the commission of the felony "set in motion" a series of events that foreseeably led to the fatal result. AI Note: This exact phrasing ("set in motion") is central to the State's argument against your client. Prepare to distinguish based on "foreseeability."

When a person embarks on a dangerous felony, such as armed robbery, it is entirely foreseeable that resistance may be encountered and that deadly force may be employed by victims, bystanders, or police officers. The ensuing death is a natural and probable consequence of the felonious act.

Query Analysis

Analyzing application of "proximate cause" to felony murder involving death of a co-felon in Indiana.

The Rule

Indiana strictly applies the proximate cause theory rather than the agency theory. A felon can be charged with felony murder if their actions during the felony set in motion a chain of events leading to a death, regardless of who pulls the trigger. [Ex Parte Jenkins]

Defense Strategy (Distinguishing)

To successfully defend against this, you must attack the foreseeability link in the causal chain. If an intervening act was highly extraordinary or unforeseeable, it may break the chain of proximate cause. [Smith v. State]

Vulnerability The court heavily favors the State in armed robbery contexts, routinely finding violent intervention "foreseeable."
